Step 2 · Lesson 1 · Free

Came to believe — why the God you believe in must be real, specific, and able

The Covenant Context

Step 1 cleared the ground. It removed the management, the story, and the illusion of control. What remains when the ground is cleared is the question that every covenant God has ever addressed: now what? The person who has honestly admitted powerlessness is standing in exactly the place where God has always met His people — at the end of what they could produce for themselves, ready to encounter what only He can provide. The covenant story, from its beginning, is the story of a God who introduces Himself specifically. Not as a philosophical concept. Not as a force available for interpretation. He said to Moses: I AM THAT I AM. He said to Abraham: I am the Almighty God. He said to Israel: I am the LORD thy God which brought thee out of the land of Egypt. God consistently identifies Himself in specific terms, with specific history, with specific demonstrated ability. Step 2 is the step where the person who cleared the ground in Step 1 encounters this specific God — not a vague higher power of personal construction, but the God who has been consistent across every era of the covenant and who is able to do what Step 1 revealed cannot be done without Him. In Step 1 we named something most people spend their lives avoiding — the honest admission that self-governance had failed completely. Step 2 is where that admission finds its answer. The God this step asks us to believe in is not a nameless force. He is a God who reveals Himself — specifically, by name. Isaiah records something worth noting: "I have even called thee by thy name: I have surnamed thee, though thou hast not known me." — Isaiah 45:4 KJV . God surnames. A surname is a family name placed upon someone by another. It carries identity, ownership, belonging. The God we are being asked to believe in is the God who places His name on His people. That is the kind of God worth believing in. The God this step asks us to believe in is not a concept but a person who responds to the person who turns toward Him. The Scripture that records this turning uses a word worth knowing: repentance — the change of mind and direction that opens the door to what God has always been ready to give.

Came To Believe

The word came matters. Not: believed. Not: decided to believe. Came to believe — the language of a journey. The faith that Step 2 describes is not a switch that flips. It is a road that is walked. And the road begins exactly where the person is — not where they think they should be. “Is any thing too hard for the LORD?” — Genesis 18:14 KJV God asked this question to Abraham, in the Old Testament, in the covenant account of the promised son. The question is rhetorical. The answer is no. And the question has never been withdrawn. It is asked to every person who arrives at Step 2 carrying the weight of what they believe about their own situation: too damaged, too far gone, too long in the pattern, too specific a case for the general remedy to apply. Is anything too hard for the LORD? The question demands an honest answer. And the honest answer — based not on how things feel but on who God has consistently shown Himself to be across the entire covenant story — is no. God does not wait for a person to clean up before He reveals Himself. He appeared to Moses in the back side of the desert — at eighty years old, tending sheep that were not his own. The bush was burning in the wilderness, not the temple.

Why A Vague God Cannot Help

The higher power of personal construction — vague enough to be comfortable, broad enough to include whatever the person prefers — cannot help. Not because the intention behind it is wrong. Because the power required for the transformation Step 1 has exposed as necessary is specific, real, and not available in a concept. “For I am the LORD, I change not.” — Malachi 3:6 KJV Malachi wrote this at the close of the Old Testament — the last prophetic word before four hundred years of silence, and the declaration that everything that had been consistent about God throughout the covenant story would remain consistent. He does not change. The God who parted the sea is the same God who met the prodigal while yet a great way off is the same God who is being introduced in Step 2. The consistency across the covenant arc is the ground of the belief. A vague higher power cannot offer this consistency because it has no history. The God of Scripture has one — and that history is the evidence that Step 2 is asking the student to examine.

Two Requirements For Coming To God

“But without faith it is impossible to please him: for he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him.” — Hebrews 11:6 KJV Two things are required for the person who comes to God. First: believing that He is — that He exists, that He is real, that He is specific. Not a concept. Not a projection. An actual being who is present and active. Second: believing that He rewards those who diligently seek Him — that seeking Him produces something, that the approach is met with a response, that the God who initiates the covenant also responds to the person who walks toward Him. The person at Step 2 does not need to have both of these at full strength. Hebrews 11:6 describes the direction of travel, not the required starting point. Mark 9:24 records an honest prayer that has been prayed by every person who has arrived at this step: Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief. That prayer is the beginning of the journey that Hebrews 11:6 describes.

The Road Forward

This is the consistent pattern of Scripture: God reveals Himself specifically, not generically. He told Moses His name. He told the Samaritan woman everything she had ever done. He is not a concept. He is a person who knows the specific person He is addressing. Step 2 L1 has introduced the question — is anything too hard? — and the two requirements for coming to God. Lesson 2 deepens the encounter with the specific God who meets that question: His consistency across the covenant, His demonstrated ability, and why the belief Step 2 describes is not a feeling but a decision grounded in evidence.

The Death That Produces Life

“Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit.” — John 12:24 KJV Jesus said this about Himself — describing what His death would produce. But the principle He named is the same principle that Step 1 enacted and Step 2 now stands at the threshold of. The corn of wheat that does not fall into the ground and die remains alone. It does not multiply. It does not change. It stays exactly as it was — a single seed, intact, producing nothing. The old life — the management, the hiding, the self-governance, the flesh running itself — had to die for what Step 2 is pointing toward to become possible. Not weakened. Not reformed. Dead. The admission of Step 1 was that death — the acknowledgment that the self-governing life had reached its end and could not produce what only God provides. What comes next is not improvement of the old life. It is the life that emerges from the death of the old one. “Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life.” — Romans 6:4 KJV Paul wrote this to born-again believers at Rome — describing what the death of the old life and the emergence of the new one looks like. Buried. Raised. Walking in newness of life. Not the old life improved. Not the old life managed. A new life that emerged from the burial of what could not produce what God requires. Step 2 is the first step of that new life — the coming to believe in the God who raises what was dead. The person standing at Step 2 has left the old life in Step 1. The question now is whether they will believe in the God who has something entirely different to give them on the other side.

Bethel — The Encounter That Changes Identity

Jacob left home with nothing but his own plans and his own history — a man who had deceived his way through every significant moment of his life, running from what his choices had produced. He slept in the open with a stone for a pillow. And there, in that specific place of his lowest point, God met him. “And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it. And, behold, the LORD stood above it, and said, I am the LORD God of Abraham thy father, and the God of Isaac.” — Genesis 28:12-13 KJV God did not meet Jacob after he had cleaned up his history. He met him on the road out of his worst decision, at a random stopping point with a rock for a pillow. And the encounter changed the name of the place — Jacob called it Bethel, the house of God — and it marked the beginning of the change of the man. Recovery needs defining encounters — not only gradual effort. The person who is accumulating program time without a genuine encounter with the God the program points toward is in Bethel with their eyes closed. The stone is under their head. The ladder is there. The question is whether the encounter has happened. What has God interrupted to meet you? Name the stone you were sleeping on. The person who leaves Step 2 having genuinely come to believe is not the same person who arrived. Something has shifted — not in the circumstances, but in what they are oriented toward. Belief, when it is real, reorders everything that follows. Belief without a corresponding decision remains exactly where it started. What Step 3 requires is the moment belief becomes a choice.
